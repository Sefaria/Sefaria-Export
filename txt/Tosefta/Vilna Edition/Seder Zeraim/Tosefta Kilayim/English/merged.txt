Tosefta Kilayim



Chapter 1

All the pairs (e.g., wheat and <i>z'vanin</i>, and the four other matching pairs set forth in Kilayim 1:1) that the Sages counted as being the same species as one another are not kilayim (diverse kinds) with each other, and the remaining field vegetables and garden vegetables that are the same species as one another are [also] not kilayim with each other.
Anise and <i>gof'nin</i> (a type of dill), coriander and parsley: Even though they resemble each other, they are kilayim with each other. Cucumbers and gourds and watermelons and melons are not kilayim with each other, and terumah and tithes may be separated for one on behalf of the other, the words of Rabbi Meir. Rabbi Yehuda and Rabbi Shimon say, they are kilayim with each other, and terumah and tithes may not be separated for one on behalf of the other. Rabbi Akiva added garlic and wild garlic, and onion and dwarf-onion, and <i>tormos</i> and <i>p'loslos</i> (two types of lupines).  Said Rabbi Shimon, Rabbi Akiva only taught about two of those pairs; however, <i>tormos</i> and <i>p'loslos</i> are not kilayim with each other.
In the district of Ariach they would graft apples onto medlars. A student found them. He said to them, "You are forbidden." They went and cut them down, and they came and asked [the Sages] in Yavneh. They said, "The student spoke well." In the marketplace of Sepphoris they would graft quinces onto pears. A student found them. He said to them, "You are forbidden." They went and cut them down, and they came and asked [the Sages] in Yavneh. They said, "Who injured you?" He was none other than one of the students of Shammai.
Rabbi Nehemiah says that Aramaic, Egyptian and Greek gourds are hybrids with the <i>ramusa</i>. Five things were stated about a Greek gourd: It is forbidden [to permit it] to cover grains; its stalk is one handbreadth; the smallest amount of it makes [other foods mixed with it] forbidden (i.e., in the event that the gourd itself is forbidden for any reason); it brings in impurity and blocks impurity<sup>*</sup><i class="footnote">per Guggenheimer, "If the leaves of Greek gourd are growing on a trellis, they act as roof. A human corpse in a house brings impurity on everything in the house. On the other hand, the roof of the house and anything above it are free from impurity. If the corpse is not in a house, anybody who would step over it would create a temporary tent and hence would become impure."</i>; and [as to] one who makes a vow not to benefit from gourds, only the Greek gourd is forbidden.
A chicken, a peacock and a pheasant: Although they resemble one another, they are kilayim with one another. Rabbi Yehuda says,  [in regards to] a female mule in heat, one may not [attempt to] mate a horse or a donkey with her, but only a mule. An ox and a wild ox, a donkey and a wild ass, a pig and a wild boar: Although they resemble one another, they are kilayim with one another.
Five things were stated about a wild ox: It is forbidden [to slaughter on the same day as its calf] on account of "it and its offspring," and its fats are are forbidden, and it may be sacrificed on top of the Altar, and it is liable for the priestly gifts (i.e., the shankbone, cheeks, and maw), and it may be purchased with [second] tithe money for purposes of [bringing it as] a peace offering, but not for "meat for one's craving" (see Deut. 12:20). And behold, it is like a domesticated animal in every respect. Rabbi Yosei says, that is the <i>t'o</i> spoken of in the Torah (Deut. 14:5), and it is permitted on account of "it and its offspring," and its fats are permitted, and it is disqualified from being sacrificed on top of the Altar, and it is exempt from the priestly gifts, and it may be purchased with [second] tithe money for "meat of one's craving," but not for [purposes of] bringing as a peace offering, and behold, it is like a wild animal in every respect. And the Sages say, a <i>t'o</i> is a creation in and of itself, and a wild ox is a creation in and of itself.
They may not plant a pomegranate shoot, nor pomegranate stems, on the side of a trunk of a sycamore tree; and they may not place a grape-vine between two walls because it will bear fruit between them; and they may not place two kinds [of produce] inside one reed tube because they hybridize with one another; and they may not graft olives onto the branch of a date palm, because that is a tree with a tree; and they may not graft cuscuta on top of <i>azmazma</i> (a prickly shrub), nor <i>tarad</i> (a species of beet) on top of amaranthus, because that is a vegetable with a vegetable. 
They may not graft cuscuta on top of a fig tree, because that is a vegetable with a a tree. [But] Rabbi Yehuda permits a vegetable with a tree. [In regards to] a tree with a vegetable<sup>*</sup><i class="footnote">i.e., a shoot from a fruit tree grafted onto a vegetable plant</i>, Rabban Shimon ben Gamaliel permits it in the name of Rabbi Yehuda ben Agra of K'far Akko.  
One who places a small piece<sup>*</sup><i class="footnote"><b>מעה</b> not <b>מינה</b> ("a kind") (MS Vienna, MS Erfurt, Lieberman)</i> of lupine (<i>tormus</i>) on the side of a small piece of a gourd, in order for it to sharpen the [fecundity of the] earth in favor of [the gourd], is liable. One plants anything that promotes growth on top of marshes or <i>chilat</i> (a type of reed) is liable. On top of a stone or on top of spring of water is exempt. And [in regards to] something that does not promote growth, whether on top of marshes, or on top of <i>chilat</i>, or on top of stone, or on op of a spring of water, is exempt.
One who sows, and one who weeds, and one who covers up [diverse kinds with dirt] violates a negative commandment. Rabbi Akiva says, even one who maintains [diverse kinds in his field] violates a negative commandment, and those who sow [produce] and those who plant trees are one and the same. One who sows grape-kernels together with wheat: Behold, that one is administered forty lashes.

Chapter 5




A horse may not be tied to a mule, or a mule to a donkey, or a donkey to a wild ass, because this one may be driven with that one. If he [nonetheless] did so, Rabbi Meir holds him liable, and the Sages exempt him. Rabbi Yehuda says, the offspring of a female horse and the offspring of a female donkey are forbidden [to be tied] together, but the sages say, all mules are a single species. Assi the Babylonian says, it is forbidden to ride on the back of a mule, from a <i>kol v'chomer</i>: Just as where it is permitted to wear two cloaks on top of one another [even if one is made of wool and the other of linen], yet it is forbidden [to wear a single cloak] if it is of a forbidden mixture, thus where it is forbidden to drive two [diverse species] together, does it not all the more so stand to reason that a forbidden mixture of both them should be forbidden? They said to him, behold, it says (1 Kings 1:33, JPS tr.), "and have my son Solomon ride on my mule, and bring him down to Gihon." He said to them, one cannot derive [halacha] from [actions performed] in urgency.  They said to him, it says [as to Solomon] (2 Kings 18:3), "He did what was correct in the eyes of God, like all that his father David had done, and [as to David] (1 Kings 15:5), "He never turned throughout his life from all that had been commanded him [except in the matter of Uriah the Hittite]."
A dog is a species of domesticated animal, [and] Rabbi Meir says, it is a species of wild animal.<sup>*</sup><i class="footnote">The printed text reads, "A dog is a species of <i>wild</i> animal, and Rabbi Meir says, it is a species of <i>domesticated</i> animal." However, these positions are opposed to the positions in the following two sentences, and the manuscripts and commentators differ as to which position should be ascribed to Rabbi Meir and the Sages. This emendation follows the more conservative approach, which is also consistent with the final halacha re: the village dog (see Minchat Bikkurim).</i> What [practical] difference is there between [the opinions of] Rabbi Meir and the Sages? Rather, one who writes over [a document gifting] his "wild animals" to his son, Rabbi Meir says he did write over his dog (i.e., include his dog in the gift), and the Sages say, he did not write over his dog.  One who writes over his "domesticated animals" to  his son, Rabbi Meir says he did not write over his dog, and the Sages say, he did write over his dog. [But the Sages agree that] a village (כופרי) dog is a wild animal.
