Verse 22

Translation:
“And if you err and do not perform all these commandments…” (Num. 15:22) until “And the children of Israel were in the wilderness” (v. 32).
The offerings for unintentional sins were already presented in Parashat Vayikra (Leviticus 4), so it is proper to examine:
	•	Why are they repeated here?
	•	What is their relevance in this section, coming after the episode of the spies?
Also, there (in Leviticus), Scripture said:
“When a person sins unintentionally by violating any of the commandments of the Lord that should not be done…”
But here it says:
“And you do not do all these commandments”—which suggests that in Leviticus, the focus is on violations of prohibitions (negative commandments), while here, the reference is to positive commandments.
Furthermore, there, Moses is not mentioned at all. But here, it says:
“All the commandments that the Lord commanded Moses…”
Also, in Leviticus, all categories of sinners are mentioned:
	•	If the anointed priest sins,
	•	If the entire community sins,
	•	If a leader sins,
	•	If an individual sins—four categories.
But here, only two levels are mentioned: the community and the individual.
Another difference:
	•	In Leviticus, for the community’s unintentional sin, God commanded a bull for a sin offering.
	•	But here, for the community, a bull for a burnt offering and a male goat for a sin offering are required—something not mentioned there.
Also, in Leviticus, the section is addressed only to Israelites—not a word about the convert (ger).
But here, the convert is mentioned repeatedly, e.g.:
“It shall be forgiven to all the congregation of Israel, and to the convert who dwells among them…”
Moreover, Leviticus speaks only of unintentional sins (shogeg) and says nothing at all about intentional sins (mezid)—even though it is understood that anyone who is liable to bring a sin offering for an unintentional transgression is liable to karet (spiritual excision) for the same act done intentionally.
But here, after speaking of unintentional sin, Scripture goes on to say:
“But the person who acts defiantly…”
So, you now have eight key differences between what is stated here and what is stated in Leviticus.
⸻
Because of these distinctions, our Sages, of blessed memory, explained that this section speaks about the unintentional sin of idolatry (avodah zarah). That is:
	•	In Leviticus, the Torah speaks about violating individual prohibitions unintentionally.
	•	That’s why it says:
“Doing one of the commandments that should not be done…”
But here, it doesn’t say that—because idolatry is a sin that undermines the entire Torah.
Ramban wrote that this section deals with the offering for one who has completely left the Torah, either as an individual or as a community. For example:
	•	An individual who has joined a gentile nation and no longer considers himself part of Israel.
	•	Or a community that mistakenly thinks the Torah is no longer binding.
This might occur in the case of a captive Jewish child raised among non-Jews (tinok shenishba), or a community that thinks the Torah was temporary and not eternal.
Their explanation is correct, and based on their view, one can explain all eight differences.
⸻
But according to the plain meaning (peshat):
	•	After the generation of the spies doubted Moses and his prophecies, and did not believe in his words,
	•	And Korah and his followers questioned his legitimacy,
	•	This section appears here to teach that anyone who doubts the Torah of Moses being divine, even unintentionally, must bring a special offering to atone for this doubt.
If the doubt was intentional—as with the spies and Korah’s faction—they will be cut off from their people.
Thus, this is why this passage is placed between the story of the spies and that of Korah. And this is why the commandment is repeated here, even though it already appeared in Leviticus:
	•	Leviticus discusses offerings for general unintentional sins;
	•	Here, it refers specifically to someone who says, “The Torah is not from Heaven,” or that “Moses made it up himself.”
That is the first difference mentioned above.
⸻
And because Leviticus discusses sins done through error, it says:
“Who does one of the commandments that should not be done…”
But here it says:
“You did not perform all these commandments…”
—because someone who denies the Torah from Heaven doesn’t deny one commandment, but all of them. That’s the second difference.
And because here it refers to someone who says that Moses invented the Torah, Scripture says:
“All that the Lord commanded Moses… through Moses…”
—to refute that view.
⸻
The passage mentions both written and oral law:
	•	“That the Lord commanded Moses” = the oral law,
	•	“That the Lord commanded you through Moses” = the written law.
Hence it says:
“From the day the Lord commanded… for your generations”—to include all commandments given in Egypt, Sinai, Marah, and the Tent of Meeting. This is the third difference.
⸻
Leviticus lists all categories of sinners because anyone can err unintentionally.
But here, it doesn’t mention the anointed priest or the leader, because it’s inconceivable that they would say, “There is no Torah from Heaven.” So only the individual or the community are listed. That’s the fourth difference.
⸻
Also, in Leviticus, the sin offering is a bull, but no burnt offering is required, because the community wasn’t distanced from God.
But here, the sin is much worse—denying the divine origin of the Torah.
	•	So they must bring a male goat (a despised animal) for a sin offering
	•	And a bull for a burnt offering to reattach themselves to God.
These are the fifth and sixth differences.
⸻
In Leviticus, no mention is made of the convert (ger) because belief was already expected.
But here, converts were often the first to lack faith, and even mislead Israelites—like a contagious skin disease—so the Torah includes them:
“It shall be forgiven to the whole congregation and to the convert among them…”
Even one raised from birth in idolatry, if he sinned unintentionally, can be forgiven—if he brings an offering and repents. That’s the seventh difference.
⸻
Finally, in Leviticus, the Torah doesn’t mention the punishment for intentional sin, because the focus is on the sacrifices.
But here, since the Torah wants to emphasize the severity of denying Torah from Heaven, it explicitly describes the punishment for the one who acts with a high hand (defiantly)—he is cut off, regardless of whether he is a native-born Israelite or a convert.
This proves the eighth difference.
⸻
Thus, all eight differences between the section in Leviticus and the one here in Numbers 15 have been explained, and the difficulty is resolved.
