2. A Non-Jew Should Taste It.  It is written in the Drisha that since a Jew is forbidden to taste it, it follows that when buying a peice of meat that might not be salted it would be forbidden to lick it to taste for whether it was salted or not.  However we see in Siman 42 concerning a gallbladder that it is recommended to to lick it to taste for blood.  Therefore, we must say that licking to taste doesnt constitute a problem if we are dealing with a doubtful situation.  Therefore, by a gallbladder it would be permitted to lick for taste as written in the Beit Yosef quoting the Mordechai. The issue that arises here from the mixutre of forbidden fats and kosher meat is different in that there needs to be an actual consumption of the mixture to ascertain whether there is flavor of the prohitted item, therefore we require the tasting of a non-jew.  Bolstering this distinction, we see in the laws of Fasting O''C 567 that licking to taste is permitted as long as there isnt a full on comsuption.  When the S'A says to have a non-jew taste, the assumption is that it is even the usage of a non- 'Kfeila (professional chef)' based on the Beit Yosef's understanding of the expression used by the Raavad, in the laws of forbidden foods, of 'non-jew' to the exclusion of 'Kfeila'.  If so, it must be that the mechanism of usability of the non-jewish non-chef as a taste barometer is the concept of 'Mesiach Le'fi Tumo' - 'story telling' or 'innocently', that is: without being prompted with the knowledge that his answer will dictate the permissibility or prohibtion of the questionable mixture at hand. See the Beit Yosef in the name of the Rashba.  The Ra'ah asks in the Bedek Habayit: "Mesiach Le'fi Tumo' is only applied when dealing with the integrity status of a women in captivity as it serves a clarification of events. The Terumat Hadeshen shares this quandry in Siman 79, to which he answers that the whether 'the taste of something being its essence - Taam Kikar' is not a biblical prohibition, therefore, in categories of rabbinic prohibitions 'Mesiach le'fi tumo' would suffice to clarify the doubt at hand.  The Beit Yosef writes in the name of the Torat Habayit Extended Version that he agrees with Rabbeinu Tam (who holds that Taam Kikar is Biblical) which could be limited to that case law in discussion, but would disagree to the biblical nature of Taam K'Ikar and rather follows the opinions of Rashi and Rambam that it is of a Rabbinic nature.  Perhaps this line of thought is what the Beit Yosef is following when he allows relying on Rambam and Rashba for trusting a non-jewish non-chef speaking innocently.  However, this point of contention is already discussed in the decisions of the Ra'i siman 130 regarding establishing the status of a first born animal. The Ra'i quotes the Rosh that a non-jew inncently relating that the animal in question is a first born is believed as long as the statement doesn't increase the value of the animal.  On this the Ra'i asks that we should only limit the trustworthiness of a non-jew to cases of women, as mentioned above.  To this he answers that according to the Rashba and the Rosh as found in the chapter of "HaGozel",  the inability to apply 'Mesiach Lefi Tumo' to Biblical probitions is for required testimony, unlike the clarification nature of testimony in 'Issur V'Heter', as we do find that a single witness is believed to permit the prohibited and therefore 'Mesiach Lefi Tumo' is sufficient.  This also fits well as we see that a non-jewish professional chef is believed even if asked directly, which would be prohibited if applied the biblical nature of testimony to 'Issur V'Heter' and not clarification nature of the testimony.  However, at the end of the day the S'A 316 decided that we don't rely on innocent storytelling in establishing the first-born status of an animal and that 'Mesiah Lefi Tumo' is insufficient when the issue at hand is Biblical.     The Tur writes: "So does my father the Rosh write: 'It is required...'"  The B'Y understands the Tur's quote of the Rosh is unclear whether 'Kfeila' is needed or not.  This is difficult to understand as the Rosh himself is very careful with his words and since he specifically mentions 'Professional Chef' we can exclude a non-jewish non-chef.  What would follow according to the Rosh is that if the non-jew knew we were relying on his statement for something other than 'Issur V'Heter' it is considered 'Lefi Tumo' unlike other cases (testimony of a woman) which 'Lefi Tumo' doesn't help. 
 <b>If one type [of food] was mixed with its own type:</b> The conclusion of the decisors is that a type that was mixed with its own type is nullified from the Torah with just a majority, as it is stated (Exodus 23:2), "to follow the majority." However rabbinically, they required sixty [times as much as what is not kosher]. Hence when there is a doubt, such as that which spilled out, we are lenient and say that there was sixty there. However with a type of food in something that is not its own type, it requires sixty from the Torah, since we [otherwise] feel the taste of that which is forbidden in that which is permissible. Hence when there is a doubt, we are stringent.




As a preface to we will explain the difference between the Beit Yosef and the Rama in understanding the Tur.  This is the verbiage of the Tur: "...And if the amount of the Issur is known, e.g., A new spoon that is used to stir one olive's worth of milk and is subsequently used to stir a meat dish within 24 hours, then 60 times the volume of only the one olive's worth that was absorbed is needed to nullify the prohibited mixture.  However, an old spoon used many times to stir an indeterminate volume requires a nullification of 60 times the volume of the whole spoon."

Siman 99

<b>"Forbidden bones join in (with the permitted food)."</b><br><br>This is because the forbidden bones, although not fit for consumption themselves, still absorb the forbidden taste through cooking. Thus, they are considered to join in and nullify the forbidden food since the forbidden flavor spreads into them. Furthermore, these bones also contain moisture that could confuse the forbidden taste absorbed from the forbidden meat.<br><br><b>The question of the Magen Avraham</b> (<i>M"A</i>) on the <b>Rema</b> (<i>R. Moses Isserles</i>), who stated in <b>Talmud Torah</b> that even clay vessels require 60 times their volume against the forbidden substance, is that the bones should be considered like the clay vessels in this context. The question is why forbidden bones are not treated like a piece of meat that is attached to the forbidden meat, which is treated as having a nullification of 60 times its volume.<br><br><b>The response</b> is that the bone is comparable to a utensil. According to the ruling in <b>Shulchan Arukh</b>, a utensil does not require a 60 times volume nullification if it is a metal utensil, according to the opinions of the <b>Mordechai</b>. Since the bone can be separated from the forbidden meat, and we follow the ruling that utensils of bone can be purged through immersion (<i>hag’alah</i>), the bone should be treated similarly, and it is considered to join with the permitted food.<br><br>Therefore, in this case, the bone does not require 60 times its volume in the mixture and is considered to join with the permitted food.<br><br><i>This explanation clarifies the stance that although forbidden bones themselves are not edible, they still participate in the nullification of the forbidden taste they have absorbed, similar to how utensils can be purified through specific methods.</i>

"All the images, etc." — In our Mishnah, Rabbi Meir and the Sages disagree. Rabbi Meir holds that since a certain image was worshiped in one place, we decree other places because of it. But the Sages do not make such a decree unless the image has in it's hand a sword, etc., as written here in the Shulchan Aruch.  Nevertheless, it seems that even if the image has a sword, this does not suffice to prohibit it unless it is a type of image that we know is actually worshiped—similar to Rabbi Meir's prohibition, which is based on a decree. Therefore, there is no distinction between whether the image is of a person or of some other form.  And in the Tur, it is written later in this same section: "a human form standing, etc., and holding a staff, etc." And the Beit Yosef (B.Y.) comments here that the implication from his words is that the prohibition applies specifically to a human form, but not to other kinds of images. His intention seems to be that, in the case of a human form, the reason is it is known that people worship it—unlike other forms. However, if it were clearly known that people do worship other forms as well, then the same law would apply to them too.  For that reason, the Rambam wrote—and the Shulchan Aruch copied here—"and the image has in its hand..." without mentioning that it must be a human form, for the reason we stated above. It seems that what is said in the Gemara: "Rav Yehuda said in the name of Shmuel: [This refers to] the statue (Andarti) of kings"—there too, he is teaching about this when it is known that the image is worshiped, as Rashi explains. And my father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote that what the Shulchan Aruch ruled—that it simply mentions "an image"—is incorrect. But this is difficult: is it really his own (original) opinion? For it is the Rambam's ruling in Chapter 7 of Hilchot Avodat Kochavim (Laws of Idolatry), and the intention is as we have explained....
That by default they are assumed to me made for idolatry.... The Tur writes about this that since it is a case of possible idol worship, it is forbidden, because by default, etc... But this is difficult to understand: since it is assumed to be so (i.e., for idol worship), why does he need to categorize it as a doubt and rule stringently? And further, it is difficult what he says afterward, regarding large cities, that it is certainly made for ornamentation, why should this be considered definite? For it is also possible that it was made for idol worship! And it seems that even though in cases of doubt regarding idol worship we are stringent, nevertheless, whenever there is a reason to permit and also a reason to forbid, it is fitting to permit — for we hold in all places that we do not presume something is forbidden out of doubt. And we are stringent in cases of doubt only where a prohibition had previously been established. Therefore, since there is a reason to say that the object was made for ornamentation, we do not presume this [other] reason to establish it definitively [as made for idol worship], since there is supporting logic from the principle that we do not assume something is forbidden. However, in villages, where it is not the usual practice to make such items for ornamentation, if so the assumption that it was made for idol worship is equal to the argument for permissibility — for both have weaknesses: the prohibition has a weakness that we do not assume prohibition out of doubt, and the permissiveness has a weakness that it is not the normal way to make such things for ornamentation. Thus, the two sides are equal, and since it remains a doubt, we rule stringently in a case involving idol worship. And my father-in-law (the Bach), of blessed memory, wrote that the understanding of the Tur is not to consider it a double doubt — a doubt whether it was made for idol worship, and if it was, perhaps it was nullified, that this is difficult, because there is no nullification in this case; even dragging it would not be considered nullification as long as its form has not been diminished. And here, we are speaking of intact items. For if they were broken, then indeed it would be nullified, as is written later.




"Like four faces." One should not ask: "Doesn't it say afterwards that even a single human form is prohibited?" The Ran answers: here it’s worse (it's not a complete human), because he combined other forms with the human form, and you might have thought it was permitted, since it’s not a pure human form. And likewise with the ministering angels, since they have wings, (you might have thought it doesn’t count as a human form), Therefore it teaches us (that even these are prohibited). But it's difficult, if so, if one were to make a human form with only one or two of the four faces, would that then be permitted because it’s not a full human form and not a full four-faced form? That doesn't seem reasonable to permit, because such a novel idea surely would not have been left unsaid by the halachic authorities, they would have told us it’s allowed. Rather, it seems there's a distinction: one because of “Eti” (with me), that this refers to ministers above, eg the four faces together, and one because of "Oti" (of me- God), that this is the form of man alone.  If it were only based on “Oti” (the human form alone), one might have thought that after erasing or damaging the human part, it’s now permitted to keep the object, since the prohibition was only due to the human image. Therefore, it comes to teach us through the “Eti” derasha that there is a separate prohibition from the presence of the four faces. And if so, all the faces are prohibited, and it doesn’t help to remove just the human face, because the rest still fall under prohibition. Therefore, one would have to erase all the forms to fix the issue—this is the correct approach. Then I saw in the Semak (Sefer Mitzvot Katan) siman 160, and this is what he writes: "The author asks: why don't we learn out the prohibition from the human form alone? The answer: this is only needed when a human form already exists, and one completes it by adding the other three faces (which are otherwise permitted to make alone)." But one cannot answer that there is no prohibition of a human form unless it’s in full bodily form with all limbs, as the Shulchan Aruch says in section 7, and therefore if it’s only the face it should be allowed; however, when it comes to the four faces, there is a prohibition even if it’s just the four faces alone [without bodies], because if so the question doesn’t make sense, why would it ask, "Then just the face of a man should be permitted," if in fact the face of a man is permitted [as you claim]? Rather, it must be that all four faces are depicted in their complete form, with full bodies, as seen in the Divine Chariot.




"It is permitted to make them." — This implies that all the prohibited forms mentioned above are permitted to make when they are sunken. However, the Tur later brings in the name of the Ramban, who wrote that anything prohibited by the Torah due to the verse “You shall not make with Me” — that is, the four faces from the dwelling of the divine presence, images of angels from the upper realm, and the forms of the sun, moon, stars, and constellations from the lower realm, and the form of a human being because it says “You shall not make Me,” which means: in the likeness through which I reveal Myself to the prophets — all of these are forbidden whether they are sunken or raised.  However, for other forms, there is also a prohibition against making them, but it is rabbinic. In those cases, there is a difference between sunken and raised forms. Consequently, even regarding those which are biblically prohibited to make — but their possession is rabbinically prohibited due to suspicion — there is still a distinction between sunken and raised.  This opinion is brought by the Ran in the name of earlier authorities, and he writes that this is the primary opinion, and he proves it from the Talmud. If so, it is surprising that the Shulchan Aruch did not bring the Ramban’s opinion.  As for practical halacha, certainly one should not be lenient in opposition to the view of the Ramban, and this is indeed the conclusion of the Tur, as well as the Ran. And my father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote that according to the Ramban, in cases where the Torah prohibits making the image, but possessing it is prohibited rabbinically, even possessing such an image in sunken form is forbidden. However, in the Ran’s own words — which he attributes to the Ramban’s opinion — it is explicitly written that possessing them is permitted if they are sunken. And regarding what seems implied in the Ramban’s words, that even other forms are prohibited to make rabbinically — the Ran explains that this refers to forms that were commonly used by idolaters in worship. But if there is no such custom to worship them, then making them is permitted, as stated in the Shulchan Aruch, section 6.

"We do not cause their death" — In Chapter "Eilu Metziot" (26a), Rabbi Abahu teaches: "Gentiles and shepherds of small livestock — we do not raise them nor lower them." The phrase "nor lower them" means that it is forbidden to lower them (actively cause their death). This is also stated explicitly in the Talmud, in Chapter "Arba Mitot" (Sanhedrin 56a), where it says regarding a Jew who kills a gentile: he is exempt from punishment. The Talmud explains that it cannot say "permitted" because we have the principle of "we do not raise them nor lower them." Rashi explains: "We do not save them from the pit or death, but we also do not lower them into the pit to kill them actively." Tosafot ruled this straightforwardly in the first chapter of Avodah Zarah (10b), where they asked about Antoninus, who killed his slaves, noting that even for Jews, it would be forbidden due to the principle of "not lowering."
The Beit Yosef wrote that "not lowering" does not mean it is forbidden to lower them but rather that there is no obligation to do so, and he understood this from Tosafot. However, he overlooked the complete discussion in "Arba Mitot." What the Beit Yosef cited from Tosafot comes from Avodah Zarah (26a), where they explain that even though gentiles do not observe the seven Noahide laws, the verse includes them for the sake of permission: "They shall be your slaves and serve you." This means there is no obligation to kill them. The Beit Yosef understood from Tosafot's use of the word "permission" that "not lowering" means it is optional, not prohibited. But this is incorrect — rather, the point is that there is no biblical command to eliminate them, but the sages prohibited actively lowering them into danger.
The Beit Yosef also brought evidence from Rambam, who wrote that one should not cause their death and that it is forbidden to save them, as quoted in the Shulchan Aruch. The Beit Yosef inferred that since Rambam did not use the word "forbidden" in the first part, it implies there is no prohibition, just no obligation. And indeed, Rambam initially wrote that it is a mitzvah to cause the death of heretics and informers, but for gentiles, there is no mitzvah to cause their death — but if one wants to, they are permitted. However, this is unrelated to the Talmudic rule of "not lowering." For direct actions (like pushing someone into a pit), it is prohibited, but indirect causation (like removing a ladder) is not prohibited, since it’s not an active action. The Tur also explained this explicitly.
Nonetheless, the Tur wrote that one may not lower them into a pit directly, but withholding rescue — like taking away a ladder — is certainly allowed. My father-in-law, of blessed memory, wrote that taking the ladder is also considered an action, based on Rambam’s phrase "one should not cause their death," implying an active prohibition. But this is not accurate: if removing the ladder were considered active killing, even a Jew doing this to another Jew would be liable for murder, yet Rambam explicitly rules (Laws of Murder, Ch. 3) that if one removes a ladder and a Jew dies, the remover is exempt. So, for gentiles, active killing is prohibited, but indirect causation — like taking the ladder — is permissible unless there is a concern for hostility ("eivah"), as will be explained.
The Talmud in "Eilu Metziot" adds: For informers and oppressors, we lower them but do not raise them. The Gemara asks: If we actively lower them, why would we even consider raising them? Rav Sheshet explains: The point is that if they begin to climb out of the pit, one may drag them back down, justifying it by saying, "I am removing the ramp to prevent my animal from falling." Rashi explains that "not raising them" means not allowing them to climb out.
At first glance, this explanation seems problematic — if lowering them is allowed, of course, preventing them from climbing out should be! But the point is that we might have thought that due to potential hostility, raising them would be permitted. The Gemara clarifies that there’s no concern for hostility in such cases, as one can use an excuse like protecting their animal.
This idea parallels Rav Yosef’s statement earlier: He initially thought that it’s permitted to save a gentile for pay to prevent hostility. Abaye countered: One can avoid hostility with excuses like "my son is on the roof." The Tur did not mention "not raising" in the case of heretics and informers, even though the baraita states it, because the main point is to teach that there’s no concern for hostility in these cases. The Tur already gave a general rule: Whenever hostility is a concern, one should act to avoid danger, but if one can evade the situation through excuses, they should do so.
In summary, the Taz concludes that the prohibition is only on direct actions that cause death. Indirect causation, like removing a ladder, is permitted unless hostility is a factor. The essence of the Talmudic teaching is balancing passive non-rescue with avoiding unnecessary enmity, all while distinguishing between active harm and indirect causation.

« “L’étude de la Torah est plus grande”, etc. — Cela est appris de Yaakov, qui fut puni pour ne pas avoir accompli la mitsva d’honorer son père pendant qu’il se trouvait chez Lavan, alors qu’il ne fut pas puni pour les quatorze années durant lesquelles il étudia chez Chem et Éver. (Voir à la fin du premier chapitre de Méguila.) »

Même si son père est un méchant — on l’apprend de ce qui est dit au chapitre “Hanéhaneqîn” (Sanhédrin 85a), que le fils ne peut pas être désigné comme agent pour frapper son père, sauf dans le cas du “mésit” (celui qui incite à l’idolâtrie). Et il y en a qui disent — c’est le Tour — qui objecte à cela à partir de ce qui est dit au chapitre “Eizehou Néchèkh” (Bava Metsia 61a), à propos de celui qui a laissé une vache volée, c’est-à-dire un bien acquis par l’usure (et aussi Rachi là-bas appelle le ribit “vol”), et que tout bien déterminé (מסויים) doit être restitué à cause de l’honneur du père. Et là-bas on objecte : “Est-on obligé de respecter son père ?! Pourtant il est écrit : ‘Tu ne maudiras pas un dirigeant de ton peuple’ (Exode 22,27), ce qui exclut quelqu’un qui ne se conduit pas comme ton peuple.” Et on répond : “Il a fait téchouva mais n’a pas eu le temps de restituer avant de mourir.” Ce qui prouve que s’il n’a pas fait téchouva, on n’est pas tenu de l’honorer. Et le Beit Yossef répond que, s’il a fait téchouva, il avait l’intention de rendre la vache ; il s’avère donc que c’est comme s’il l’avait déjà rendue, et que le fils n’a rien hérité. Et il ne peut donc pas dire : ‘Elle est à moi, et je ne suis pas tenu de l’honorer avec ce qui m’appartient.’ Tandis que s’il n’a pas fait téchouva, le bien appartient au fils par héritage. Le Beit Yossef s’étend là-dessus. Mais c’est difficile, car si c’est ainsi, pourquoi le questionneur objecte-t-il à partir de ‘il ne se conduit pas comme ton peuple’ ? Il aurait pu objecter directement du fait que l’argent appartient au fils puisqu’il n’a pas fait téchouva ! De plus, si toute la question dépend de l’intention de restituer, pourquoi distingue-t-on entre bien déterminé ou non ? Même si ce n’est pas un bien défini, dès lors qu’il a eu l’intention de restituer, il devrait être tenu de le faire. C’est donc une objection valable du Tour. Et le Tour interprète la halakha du chapitre “Hanéhaneqîn” (concernant le mésit) dans le cas où le père a fait téchouva, comme le rapporte Tosafot cités par le Beit Yossef. On peut aussi dire que le Tour fait une distinction entre frapper ou maudire d’un côté, et honorer de l’autre. Autrement dit : bien qu’il soit interdit de le frapper ou de le maudire, on n’est pas pour autant tenu de l’honorer. Et ne dis pas que ce que nous disons au sujet de celui qui ne se comporte pas comme ton peuple — qu’il ne rentre pas dans le verset “Tu ne maudiras pas” — signifie qu’il est permis de le maudire, car cela veut seulement dire qu’on n’est pas puni s’il l’a fait, mais il y a malgré tout une interdiction. Et ne demande pas : pourquoi le questionneur a-t-il objecté à partir de “il ne se conduit pas comme ton peuple” alors qu’il y a malgré tout une interdiction ? On peut dire que, puisqu’il n’y a qu’un interdit pour la malédiction, il n’y a pas du tout d’obligation de l’honorer. De plus, le Ran au début de Kiddouchin, dans une réponse, distingue entre le fait de le faire souffrir et le fait de l’honorer — contrairement à ce qu’écrit le Rema au paragraphe 8, qui ne fait aucune distinction entre le faire souffrir ou ne pas l’honorer. On voit aussi dans les Tosafot sur le chapitre “Hakotèv”, dans le commentaire sur “Pri'out Halvaot” (traité Ketoubot 86a), qu’ils distinguent entre honorer et humilier le père.

C’est pourquoi il peut l’insérer, comme on l’a dit. Et même si quelqu’un pense être strict là-dessus a priori, en tout cas, a posteriori, il est évident qu’on ne doit pas invalider, comme on l’a dit.

"We do not say on the 29th of Adar" etc. The reason given (Terumat HaDeshen siman 292) is that all cases which depend on a number of days qualify for us to say part of the last day is equivalent to a full last day. This is not the case by mourning [one's parents] which in all cases requires [a full] twelve months. If one were to say, "part of it is like all of it," one would only be required to mourn a small part of the last month, and this is obviously not correct.
